A Long-Term Season Model

Team ratings from goals, xG and betting lines, turned into match prices and season outcome odds

Author

Luke Chambers

1. Introduction

Rating a team on goals alone is noisy, on xG alone ignores finishing and goalkeeping, and on betting lines alone simply copies the market. This model blends all three into attack and defence ratings, prices every remaining fixture from them, and simulates the rest of the season to put odds on the title, the top four, the top six and relegation. Every number below is recalculated in your browser as you move the sliders.

3. Data

Two sources are joined match by match:

  • Understat for each match’s goals and expected goals (xG).
  • totalcorner.com for the pre-match Asian handicap and goal line, for both the top flight and the division below. The goal line is solved for the Poisson goals rate that prices it fairly, and the handicap for the goal difference that prices it fairly under a Skellam model. Together they give the market’s expected goals for each side, called Asian goals here. This is the same odds-to-goals step used in the corner model.

Odds to expected goals for each side → code/lt/asian_lines.py

Matches played behind closed doors (March 2020 to June 2022) are left out. Home advantage is close to zero in them, and they would distort both the ratings and the home advantage factor.

4. Ratings

Each team gets an attack rating (goals it’s expected to score) and a defence rating (goals it’s expected to concede) against an average opponent. Each is a weighted average of three rolling means over the team’s last n league matches:

\text{Attack} = w_g\,\overline{\text{goals}} + w_x\,\overline{\text{xG}} + w_o\,\overline{\text{Asian}}

where w_g + w_x + w_o = 1, and the same for goals, xG and Asian goals conceded.

The window and weights were chosen by grid search. For every window from 5 to 29 matches and every weight combination, each past match was predicted using only ratings from before it. The predictions were then scored on mean absolute error (MAE) against the market’s own total and supremacy for that match. Supremacy counted for 75% of the score, since this model is mainly for finding supremacy differences. The search used seasons up to 2022/23. The winning combination was then scored on 2023/24 onwards, which the search never saw.

Grid search: find_optimal_parameters() → code/lt/optimise.py

The best combination was a 16-match window with weights of 10% goals, 10% xG and 80% odds. Out of sample, its predictions were off the market’s by an average of 0.2056 goals on the total and 0.1797 on supremacy. The heavy weight on the odds is expected, because the market already contains most of what goals and xG say about a team. The small goals and xG weights pick up recent performance the lines may not have caught up with yet.

Promoted teams

A newly promoted team has few top-flight matches to rate it on, and none at all at the start of the season. Its matches from the season it won promotion are used instead, with the market’s expected goals scaled to top-flight level. The scaling comes from the 33 past promotions in the data. For each one, the team’s average Asian goals in its first top-flight season are compared with its promotion season. On average, promoted teams’ expected goals scored fell by 24% and their expected goals conceded rose by 52%.

Only the odds-based metric can be carried over this way, because Understat has no second-division goals or xG. Within the window, goals and xG are averaged over the team’s top-flight matches. A team with no top-flight matches yet is rated on its scaled Asian goals alone. In the app, these teams are tagged P with the number of second-division matches in their window.

Promotion factors and adjustment → code/lt/promotion.py

5. From ratings to prices

A fixture’s expected goals multiply one side’s attack by the other side’s defence, relative to the league’s average defence:

\lambda_{home} = \frac{\text{Att}_{home}\times\text{Def}_{away}}{\overline{\text{Def}}}

\lambda_{away} = \frac{\text{Att}_{away}\times\text{Def}_{home}}{\overline{\text{Def}}}

Home advantage is then moved from the away side to the home side. The home team gains 9.4% of the match’s expected goals and the total stays the same. That share is the home-minus-away goal difference as a proportion of all goals in the league’s non-COVID history.

Expected goals: predict_goals() → code/lt/ratings.py

Two independent Poisson distributions turn the expected goals into a probability for every scoreline up to 10-10. Independent Poissons slightly under-price low-scoring draws, so the Dixon-Coles correction reweights 0-0, 0-1, 1-0 and 1-1 using one parameter, ρ. ρ is fitted by maximum likelihood on past scorelines, with the market’s expected goals as the inputs (ρ = -0.034). Adding up the scorelines where the home side wins, where it’s level, and where the away side wins gives the home, draw and away probabilities. Their inverses are the fair (margin-free) 1X2 odds.

Dixon-Coles scorelines and 1X2 → code/lt/dixon_coles.py

The predicted table adds each fixture’s expected points (3 × win probability + draw probability) and expected goals to the current table.

6. Simulating the season

Expected points give the most likely finishing order, but not how likely each outcome is. For that, the rest of the season is simulated thousands of times. Each fixture is drawn as a home win, draw or away win from its 1X2 probabilities, and the final positions are recorded. The share of simulations in which a team wins the league, finishes in the top 4 or top 6, or ends in the bottom 3 is its probability, and 1 / probability is its fair price.

Monte Carlo: simulate_season() → code/lt/simulate.py

7. Limitations

  • Snapshot data. This version runs on a fixed Serie A snapshot. By the cut-off, every promoted team has played at least 30 Serie A matches, so on this page the promotion adjustment only shows up with windows of more than 30. It matters most early in a season.
  • One promotion factor for everyone. Every promoted team is scaled by the same average factor, whatever its budget or squad changes. After a few top-flight matches the market’s own lines take over.
  • Squad changes. The ratings don’t react to transfers, injuries, suspensions or manager changes until those show up in results and prices.
  • Correlated inputs. Betting lines are partly built from past goals and xG, so the three metrics overlap. The weights describe what predicts the market best, not three independent signals.
  • Equal weighting inside the window. Every match in the window counts the same. Weighting recent matches more would react faster but could over-read a tough run of fixtures, unless the opposition’s strength is accounted for.
  • Simplified simulation. Simulated matches give points only, with no goals, so teams level on points are ordered at random instead of by goal difference. Ratings are also fixed for the rest of the season and don’t update after each simulated result.
  • Market fit is one yardstick. Matching the market on MAE doesn’t show the model can beat it. The corner model goes a step further and backtests against Pinnacle prices, which is the natural next step here.
